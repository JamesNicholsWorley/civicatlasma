TOWN OF LENOX - ANNUAL REPORT 2025

Report of the Board of Selectmen

‘To the Honorable Citizens of Lenox:

The five person Selectboard serves as the goal setting, long range planning
and policy making body of the town, recommending major courses of
action to the town meeting, and adopting policy directives and guidelines
which are implemented by the town manager and town staff along with
the boards, committees, and commissions of the town. Through our Town
Manager, the Selectboard exercises general supervision over all matters
affecting the interests and welfare of our community.

The Selectboard also acts as the Town’s Local Licensing Authority, hearing
petitions for new licenses, amendments to existing licenses and one-day
licenses. With a vibrant hospitality sector and community events, this makes
up a large part of the Selectboard’s routine business. In addition, the Se-
lectboard is also responsible for determining equitable use policies for town
buildings and public spaces for various cultural and community events.

The challenging, day-to-day work of keeping our community one of the
most desirable places to live and visit in the Commonwealth rests with our
Town Manager and staff. 2025 saw considerable turnover in some very
critical roles, including Town Manager, Town Clerk, Fire Chief and Police
Chief. In addition, positions in various departments and volunteers on a
variety of boards and committees also experienced turnover.

We are incredibly pleased to report that at the end of calendar year 2025,
our town staffing is stable, and those roles have been filled with very
capable, dedicated and experienced individuals who will continue to move
Lenox forward. We are fortunate in Lenox to be able to recruit and retain
talented staff who work long hours to deliver quality services to our resi-
dents. We would like to thank all of our Town staff for their hard work and
commitment to Lenox.

which perform very critical functions within our Town government. With-
out exception, these dedicated volunteers serve without any compensation
and devote many hours to our community’s betterment. We thank them
for their service and we encourage you to consider serving on a board or
committee in the future.

Report of the Town Manager

‘To the Honorable Members of the Selectboard and the citizens of Lenox:
A Solid Foundation

In preparing for this year’s annual town report, I reviewed several past
submissions to get a sense as to what information was included. It was not
a surprise to see that as far back as 2010, several of the same challenges
that face Lenox today were present then: the cost of providing staff and
retiree health insurance, the growing list of expensive capital projects and
the pressure of providing quality municipal services while moderating

tax impacts. This is the same fiscal environment that Lenox and other
communities across the Commonwealth operate in year over year and will
likely do so into the future.

Over the past several years, Lenox has taken successful steps to address
these challenges: the use of Stabilization Funds, creating an OPEB (Other
Post Employment Benefits) Trust Fund, authorizing the local option meals
tax, developing shared service models, employing conservative budget
forecasting, following sound financial management policies and utilizing
an annual capital improvement plan, adapting to changing economic
conditions.

These practices have resulted in a fiscally stable, well positioned Town to
manage these enduring challenges in a responsible and efficient manner. In
the most recent Standard & Poors “AAA” credit rating report that provides
an independent assessment on the quality of a community, it was noted that
Lenox has “[a] strong operating track record that we believe is sustainable,
given budgeting strategies and performance of local revenue, with reserves
that we expect to increase in fiscal 2026.”

Building off this foundation, accomplishments in 2025 include securing
open space at Kennedy Park, the commissioning of our new Public Safety
Complex, launching the Wastewater Treatment Plant upgrade project,
finishing downtown improvements and continued investment in our public
infrastructure such as roads and water main replacements. Less visible, but
no less critical were improvements made to our internal processes such

as time & attendance, managed technology services, and other business

—we have more work to do in 2026.

Report of the Board of Assessors

To the Honorable Office of the Select Board and Citizens of Lenox,

The primary function of the Assessing Office is to provide the
assessment of full and fair market values, i.e. “the amount a willing buyer
would pay and willing seller on the open market” in accordance with
Massachusetts General Laws and regulations, of all real and personal
property in Lenox is to equitably allocate Lenox’s annual tax levy amongst
Lenox taxpayers. We also evaluate all applications for abatement of such
property tax, motor vehicle excise tax and make the determination of

The Water Division continues to leverage grant funding to help improve

Quality drinking water is one of Lenox’s most important resources. Lenox
needs to continue planning for additional sources of water, and take
measures that help to ensure the high quality of water the residents come
to expect. The Lenox Water Treatment plant produced 201,105,850 gallons
of water this year. The Town added 9 new connections to our system and

water meters and water meter radios as needed, and four hydrants were

additional 1,000 feet of watermain was added to complete a vital loop in
our system. This redundant loop will improve water quality and ensure
reliable service for our customers.

Water is a limited resource and the conservation and protection of existing
sources needs to be a part of any municipal water system plan. Everyone
needs to be aware of his or her water consumption and take steps to

Endangered Properties: In keeping with its mission to preserve and protect

several endangered properties, including Spring Lawn (10 Kemble St., c.
1904), Sedgewick Family Farmhouse (12 East Street, c. 1780), Mahanna
Barn (50 Church Street, c. 1888), the abandoned schoolhouse (c. 1843) on
the Eastover estate, and the Loomis-Goodell house (51 Housatonic Street,
c. 1870). One property on last year’s list—the Cook Family House (a.k.a
Elijah Northrup House, Meadow Place, or Winstanley House, 114 Main St.,
c. 1790)—was removed after it was acquired by a new owner with plans to
preserve and renovate the property.

Demolition Requests: The Commission approved demolition requests for
outbuildings at 106 Cliffwood St., 42 Walker St., and 45 West Street.

Demolition Delays: A demolition delay imposed several years ago at 12
East Street remains in effect because it has not been acknowledged by the
owner despite repeated efforts to establish communication. In July, the
Commission approved a demolition delay for the house at 51 Housatonic
St., and a public meeting on the matter was held on July 28. The property
has since been sold and the Commission is awaiting a discussion with the
new owner on plans for the building.

Minimum Maintenance Bylaw: The Commission worked on a draft bylaw
calling for minimum maintenance of historically significant buildings. The
draft bylaw is similar to those in effect in other Massachusetts towns and is
aimed at preventing “demolition by neglect” when owners do not provide
sufficient maintenance to keep their properties from becoming at risk of
demolition. The draft bylaw has been discussed with senior Town officials
and department heads, as well as the Town’s outside counsel, and the
Commission hopes it will be approved at a Town meeting in 2026.

Church on the Hill Cemetery: The deteriorating condition of the cemetery
prompted the Commission to endorse an application to the Community
Preservation Committee for funding to develop a long-term plan for the
facility. The CPC declined to fund the proposal but ensuing discussions

Report of the Historic District Commission

‘To the Honorable Select Board and Citizens of Lenox:

The Lenox Historic District was established in 1975 to promote the
educational, cultural, economic and general welfare of the public through
the preservation and protection of the distinctive characteristics of
buildings and places significant in the history of Lenox, or significant for
their architecture, and the maintenance and improvement of settings for
such buildings and places and the encouragement of design compatibility
therewith.

The Historic District is administered by the Lenox Historic District
Commission, a five-member oversight board appointed by the Board of
Selectmen. The Historic District Commission applies the Historic District
Bylaw to prevent incongruous alterations.

The Historic District is characterized by a variety of streetscapes made up
of buildings of many different types, styles or designs, sizes, and settings.
Its architecture reflects a range of historical developments, from the

early period of Lenox as the county seat through the Gilded Age with its
“summer cottages” and important residential and commercial architecture,
to the late 1930s when Tanglewood furthered Lenox’s reputation as

a cultural destination. The diversity is also evident in the variety of
building types. In each period of the village’s development, and for each
architectural style, different types of buildings are found; public buildings,
simple residences and grand cottages, commercial buildings and commercial
blocks. The diversity of the Historic District is also found in the variety

of building sizes. The result is a Historic District that is characterized

by its diversity, not its uniformity. While most of the Historic District’s
architecturally and historically significant buildings were constructed by
1923, the appearance and use of many buildings in the Historic District
continued to evolve in the following decades. These changes are now an
important part of the Historic District.

The United States Constitution does not mention education. Not once.
The framers left it to the states to determine whether schooling would be a
public obligation or a private endeavor.

Massachusetts chose to clearly and boldly enshrine education.

In 1780, our Commonwealth adopted a Constitution that declared it the
duty of government to “cherish the interests of literature and the sciences”
and to support public schools. That word “cherish” is not accidental. It
implies active care, sustained attention, and moral responsibility. Education
in Massachusetts was not treated as optional infrastructure. It was
embedded as a civic promise.

That promise remains the foundation of Lenox Public Schools.

We understand that public education in Massachusetts is not simply a
service we provide; it is a constitutional duty we fulfill. John Adams, author
of the Massachusetts Constitution, argued that an informed citizenry was
vital to preserving our rights and liberties. The strength of our democracy
depends upon informed, thoughtful, capable citizens. Schools are where
that capacity is cultivated, deliberately and daily.

In Lenox, we continue to honor the spirit of what John Adams wrote 246
years ago. We remain committed to inclusive practices, early intervention
systems, expanded academic opportunities, and responsible fiscal
management. We recognize that trust is earned and must be protected.

appropriate public education to every student. When the needs of a student
exceed the programs and services we can reasonably provide in-house, we
must secure an appropriate out-of-district placement. While the tuition and
transportation costs associated with these placements impact the budget,
they ensure that students receive the specialized instruction and supports

solving, while the 5th Grade Service Club encourages leadership and civic
responsibility. Students also take part in the Morris Spelling Bee and other
academic enrichment activities.

the Massachusetts Audubon Society, culminating in a field experience at
Pleasant Valley Wildlife Sanctuary.

Fifth grade students participate in an eight-week residency with Shakespeare
& Company, working alongside professional actors and concluding with a
live performance at the Tina Packer Playhouse. These experiences foster
creativity, confidence, and teamwork.

Special Education

Lenox Public Schools provides a broad array of services for children and
youth with disabilities from ages three through twenty-two. The referral,
evaluation, and placement of students are governed by state and federal
special education laws and regulations, including the Individuals with
Disabilities Education Act (IDEA).

The district remains committed to ensuring that each eligible student
receives a Free Appropriate Public Education (RAPE) in the Least
Restrictive Environment (LRE). Currently, approximately 12 % of the
district’s student population receives special education services. This
percentage has increased slightly over the past several years, reflecting both
expanded identification efforts and evolving student needs.

English Language Education (ELE)

Lenox Public Schools is dedicated to providing high-quality educational
experiences for students whose primary language is not English. The
English Language Education staff deliver instruction designed to foster
grade-level social and academic language proficiency while supporting
mastery of Massachusetts curriculum standards.

The ELE program currently serves approximately 30 students in grades
K-12. The Department remains committed to ensuring full access to
academic content and meaningful participation in all aspects of school life.

Section 504 Services

The Department is responsible for identifying, evaluating, and supporting
students who have a physical or mental impairment that substantially limits
one of more major life activities, as defined under Section 504 of the
Rehabilitation Act of 1973.

Students who qualify receive a 504 Accommodation Plan implemented
within the general education setting. Each plan identifies the

student’s disability, the major life activities impacted, and the specific
accommodations necessary to ensure equitable access to learning.

The district currently serves approximately 77 students under Section 504.
Plans are reviewed annually to ensure continued effectiveness; however,

Integrated Preschool Program

The Integrated Preschool Program provides developmentally appropriate
experiences in a safe, nurturing, and inclusive environment for three- and
four-year-old children. The program follows an early childhood curriculum
and operates using an integrated model in which students with disabilities
learn alongside typically developing peers.

The program currently serves up to 24 students across two classrooms and
continues to promote early learning, social-emotional development, and
school readiness. During the 24-25 school year the program enrollment
was 23 students. Several seats are held each year for Pediatric Development
Center Referrals which typically come in during the middle of the school
year.

McKinney-Vento Homeless Assistance Act

The Director of Student Services also serves as the district liaison for
students identified as homeless under the McKinney-Vento Homeless
Assistance Act. Homeless children and youth are defined as individuals who
lack a fixed, regular, and adequate nighttime residence.

The district ensures that eligible students receive support and services that
provide equal access to a free and appropriate public education comparable
to that of their housed peers. In alignment with federal requirements, the
district is currently coordinating and managing transportation for students
identified as homeless to ensure school stability and to prevent disruption
to their education. Providing consistent transportation enables students to
remain in their school of origin whenever feasible, supporting academic
continuity and social-emotional well-being.

Services may also include assistance with enrollment, access to educational
programming, and coordination with community resources as needed.

important advisory role within the district.

The responsibilities of the SEPAC include:

¢ Advising the district on matters related to the education and safety of
students with disabilities

¢ Meeting regularly with school officials to participate in the planning,
development, and evaluation of special education programs

EDUCATIONAL PROGRAM

The School Committee wishes to recognize the efforts of the administra-

Lenox’s small size and deep commitment to public education has given

our schools a distinct advantage in providing all students with the personal
attention they need while also delivering the robust and innovative program
characteristic of a much larger district. Standardized test scores at both
schools are among the highest in the commonwealth and the district is the

Report of the Department of Veterans’ Services

‘To the Honorable Select Board and Citizens of Lenox:

The Veterans’ Services office serves the Veterans of Lenox and their
families. We assist veterans in processing requests for Massachusetts
Department of Veterans’ Services (DVS) Chapter 115 benefits. Chapter
115 benefits are needs based, and include temporary financial assistance,
medical reimbursement, and assistance in applying for aid from other agen-
cies, both state and federal. Veterans with a dishonorable discharge are not
eligible. Spouses and dependent children may also be eligible, if the veteran
is eligible.

The case load of Lenox Veterans’ Services constant this year. The cost to
assist veterans this year with Chapter 115 benefits was $6,348.95. Of this
amount, 75% ($4,761.71) will be reimbursed by the state, with an actual cost
to the Town of $1,587.24. This includes regular benefits, heating assistance,
and medical reimbursements.

As noted last year, the Veterans’ Service Officer (VSO) of Lenox can no
longer can assist veterans and their spouses with federal Veterans’ Admin-
istration (VA) requests. A new VA rule prevents me from filling out VA
paperwork. However, we have a list of local organizations that can assist
the veteran so that they will receive the benefits they deserve.

As noted in previous reports, one of the most underused programs avail-
able to veterans is a program that allows a Veteran to receive Medical Only
benefits, in the event their income is too high for regular benefits. Max-
imum asset limits still apply. Maximum assets are $8,400.00 single, and
$16,600.00 married. If approved, the Medical Only program reimburses
co-pays, other medical costs, and can even reimburse the cost of Medicare
Part B and some supplemental insurances. It must be noted that, Veteran
Services does not pay the bills, but reimburses medical bills paid for by the
veteran.
