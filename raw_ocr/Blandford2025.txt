The Select Board is-pleased to. submit the following report outlining the

TOWN ADMINISTRATOR

Fiscal Year 2026 was a defining year for Blandford; marked by challenge, resilience, and
meaningful progress. Throughout the year, considerable effort was dedicated not only to
addressing immediate operational needs, but also to identifying long-term priorities and building
a stronger foundation for the future. While municipal government is often measured in budgets,
projects, and regulations, this year served as a reminder that the true strength of Blandford lies in.
its people.

One of the most significant challenges faced this year was the unprecedented influx of
commercial solar and Battery Energy Storage System. These projects raised. substantial concerns
regarding public safety, environmental impacts, infrastructure, emergency response capabilities,
and preservation of Blandford’s rural character and natural resources. In response, Town boards,.
committees, volunteers, and residents devoted countless hours to reviewing complex
information, participating in public discussions, and navigating an increasingly technical
regulatory process. .

I would like to personally thank the EPSEC Committee, Planning Board, Conservation
Commission, and Select Board for their tireless work, professionalism, and dedication.
throughout this process. I would also like to recognize the residents of Blandford, whose
engagement and participation demonstrated the pride, resilience, and commitment that continue
to define this community.

Even under difficult circumstances, Blandford came together in a way that reflects the
very best of small-town government and community involvement, and it is my hope that
momentum continues as we move into Fiscal Year 2027.

Fiscal Year 2026 also brought meaningful progress in addressing several longstanding
operational and staffing needs. The Town was able to fully staff the Highway Department,
provide additional administrative support within the Asséssor’s Office, and welcome a full-time
Assistant in the Treasurer/Collector’s Office. While these may appear to be small changes, they
represent important investments in efficiency, continuity, and the long-term stability of Town
operations.

At the same time, the Town continued moving forward on several key priorities for the
community, including preventative maintenance of Town-owned properties, the Water
Department SCADA project, upgrades to audio and visual equipment to improve public
meetings, and continued progress on the library expansion project. Blandford also continued
working toward a now nearly fully funded Other Post Employment Benefits account, which
remains critical to the Town’s long-term financial stability and bond rating, In addition, the Town
anticipates dedicating renewed focus and collaboration toward evaluating the future direction of
the Fire Department building project to ensure the long-term needs of the community continue to
be thoughtfully and responsibly considered,

As a rural community of approximately 1,200 residents, Blandford faces many challenges
that often go unseen by those unfamiliar with municipal government. Despite limited staffing
and a smail tax base, the Town is still responsibie for meeting the same jegal, administrative,
financial, and operational obligations as every other municipality in Massachusetts. Much of this
work is accomplished through the dedication of employees, elected officials, volunteers, and
residents who consistently step forward because they care deeply about the Town and its future.

That willingness to work together is what continues to make Blandford special. It is
reflected in the volunteer board and committee members who dedicate countless hours of service

jeaving personal opinions aside to do what is in the best interest of the Town, the employees who
consistently go above-and beyond, and the residents who continue to support projects, events,
and initiatives that strengthen the fabric of the community.

Blandford possesses something no budget can buy: a genuine sense of community. It is
my. sincere hope that moving forward we continue to strengthen that sense of community by
recognizing that supporting Blandford means more than identifying challenges; it means
contributing time, effort, ideas, and cooperation toward meaningful solutions that move the Town
forward for the benefit of all.

Finally, I would like to personally thank Executive Assistant Joann Martin, whose hard
work, professionalism, and unwavering dedication are invaluable to the Town each and every
day. I would also like to thank Select Board members Jackie Coury, Cara Letendre, and TJ
Cousineau for their steadfast commitment to Blandford, their collaborative leadership, and their
continued support throughout the year. Together, their dedication and service continue to help
guide Blandford toward a promising Fiscal Year 2027.

Cristina C. Ferrera
Town Administrator

Ad-Hoc Advisory Committee on the Climate Leader Community Designation

The Ad-Hoc Advisory Committee.on Climate Leader Community (CLC) Designation
was ‘established by the Blandford Select Board in June 2025. The:committee, consisting of
volunteers Chuck Benson, Paul Dirschka, and Debbie Lundgren, was charged with (1)
researching the requirements, application process, and steps required to obtain the CLC
designation; (2) identifying potential financial and administrative implications forthe town in
pursuing the designation; (3) gathering examples and lessons learned from other towns seeking
or having obtained the designation; and (4) reporting findings and recommendatiotis to the town
regarding pursuit of the CLC designation.

The:committee met eight times from June ~ October 2025, and the final report was presented

to the Select Board on November 24, 2025. Key findings were as follows:

(1). Requirements to successfully attain CLC status include (a) being a Green Community in
good standing; b) establishing an advisory committee to advise the town, coordinate.
clean energy projects, and carry out the remaining four requirements for CLC
designation; .(c):committing to elimination of fossil fuel use by 2050*; (d) establishing a
plan for decarbonization of municipal buildings and vehicles by 2050; (e) adopting a zero
emissions vehicle first policy to eventually phase out gas- -powered vehicles*; and (f)
adopting the Specialized Energy Code*. “Indicates that this article must be passed by
town vote.

(2) Blandford expressed concerns about potential financial and administrative costs
associated with pursuing the CLC designation, particularly for a small town like
Blandford (pop. 1215). To address these concerns, we explored the-experiences of two
small Massachusetts towns who.attained CLC status in May 2025. These included
Ashfield (pop. 1700) and Pelham (pop. 1250). Ashfield provides extensive online
documentation of their implementation plan. Their documentation, together with frequent
conversations with Chris Mason, Green Community Coordinator for Western
Massachusetts, sufficiently addressed the town’s concerns.

(3) Our committee unanimously agreed that the potential benefits of achieving Climate:
Leader status were well worth the effort required: CLC grants of tip'to $1 million can
fund large-scale decarbonization projects, such as solar panels on Blandford’s municipal
buildings, providing. long-term financial benefits. As an example, Ashfield has recently
been. awarded a CLC grant of $870, 872 for a solar system: at its. wastewater treatment
plant and highway garage. This project is estimated to save nearly $100,000 in energy:
costs.

ASSESSORS

State law requires assessors to estimate the fair market value of all properties within their
jurisdiction. Assessed valuations are based'on “full and fair cash value” as of January 1
preceding each fiscal year. To maintain assessments at or near market value, the assessors
‘regularly compare property valuations with the selling prices of properties that have sold.

Assessment-to-sale ratio statistics are analyzed to determine the median assessment level
and maintain assessment uniformity. A secondary, and perhaps more important, function of the
Board of Assessors is to generate equitable assessments. To that end, we continually strive to
maintain an accurate property database and refine valuation tables and formulas that yield both
equitable and explainable assessments.

With this mandate comes the requirement that assessors conduct.annual revaluations.
Revaluations not only respond to the ever-changing real estate market but are opportunities for
assessors to improve the quality of property data, estimate values that more closely correspond to
the market, as evidenced by property sales, and improve the underlying valuation methods used
to estimate values. Each of these functions must be approved by the Massachusetts Department
of Revenue - DOR.

If you observe a change in your valuation, the difference can be attributed to. one or more of
the following: .

o Market appreciation

© Building permit-related construction and improvements .

e Inspections made as part of our periodic inspection program, from which the features

listed on assessment records are updated

Assessed valuations are not interided to predict what a property might sell for in the future.
By law, assessors are only concerned with what properties were worth on the assessment date —
the January 1 preceding the fiscal year of taxation. The assessment date for fiscal year 2027 is
January 1, 2026. Properties that sold in the 12 months preceding the assessment date affect fiscal
year 2026 valuations. Many factors influence what a buyer might.pay for a property, but only
those that are readily quantifiable are used in estimating your valuation. For example, your
assessment is based on features like location, land area, building area, type and grade of
construction, age and condition, and amenities like garages, swimming pools, and finished
basements. Examples of property features not considered in-your assessment include curb appeal,
home décor, and landscaping.

‘Town reverue.comes from several sources. These include state funds (the so-called “Cherry
Sheet), motor vehicle excise taxes, town-revenue (investment income, departmental fees, PILOT
payments, meals tax, etc.), enterprise income (water and internet), and amounts voted from Free
Cash or Reserve Accounts. All expenditures are voted on during town meetings based upon a
recommended budget from the Finance Committee. The actual amount of real estate tax levied
on a property is a result of the amount of money needed to be raised to cover expenses after all
other revenue is accounted for (yielding the property tax levy), This amount is then applied to the
real estate valuation to determine a tax rate (which also has to be approved by the Massachusetts
Department of Revenue), which is then applied to the assessed valuation of each property to
yield a tax bill.

After receiving the: first actual bill for the Fiscal Year, you may séek an abatement by filing
with our office. Abatement forms may be found outside of the Assessors’ office, and you must
file by the date noted on the tax bill (Applications for abatements are due on.or before the due
date for payment of the 3rd quarter bill). Assessors are prohibited by law from acting on
untimely applications and are unable to waive those requirements. If you are unsatisfied with the
Assessors’ decisions on your abatement, you may file with the State’s Appellate Tax Board
(ATB) by using the information found on the abatement form.

For additional information, please visit the Assessors page on the town’s website.
Dr. David Hopson, Chair

ENERGY PROJECT SITING & ENGAGEMENT COMMITTEE

Successful Work of the EPSEC Committee

On September 24, 2025, the Energy Project Siting & Engagement Committee (EPSEC)
‘was formed shortly after the Town pursued atemporary moratorium related to large-scale energy
development projects. .

The creation of EPSEC came in response to several major developments impacting the
Town, including the State’s new energy permitting laws, the proposed expansion of the.
Eversource substation, and an increasing influx of commercial solar.and Battery Energy Storage
System proposals. Recognizing that these changes could significantly increase development
pressure in the community, the Town assembled a diverse working group to proactively review
and strengthen local bylaws and regulations.

The committee was.tasked with evaluating and updating the Town’s Solar and BESS
bylaws, as well as creating an Energy Overlay Map, to better prepare Blandford for future project
volume while balancing energy policy, public safety, environmental protection, and preservation
of community character.

The committee met twice a month for eight months, reviewed countless state hearing and
regulatory documents, and worked collaboratively to help define what will become Blandford’s
local energy laws for the future.

The committee’s collaborative work brought together municipal leadership, board and
committee representatives, public safety officials, and residents to help ensure the Town is better
positioned to respond thoughtfully and responsibly to future energy project proposals.

BLANDFORD MUNICIPAL LIGHT PLANT

The Fiber to the Home (FTTH) project is complete and fully operational. Service
connections to Blandford homes began in the Spring of 2021. The network is owned by
Blandford. Westfield Gas & Electric (Whip City Fiber) is the Internet Service Provider (ISP),
and Network Operator (NO). There are 52.8 miles of fiber installed.

There are currently 435 customers, or approximately 88% of residences, signed up for the
internet service. In addition, there are 103 phone subscribers.

It took several years to first design the network and then make the utility poles ready to
install fiber network. Many poles had to be replaced and throughout the town existing electric
and telephone lines had to be moved to allow room for fiber to be attached to the poles. Several
areas such as turnpike crossings required custom engineering solutions and special permissions.
Sertex began the actual construction on November 30, 2020. Constructing the fiber network was
a3 step process: Installing steel strand on poles, lashing Fiber to the strand, and finally installing
MST’s. The Town is divided into five (5) Fiber Service Areas (FSA’s).

The Network Hub is located at 4 North Blandford Rd, and includes a Hut, generator and
propane backup. We currently have 10 gigs of service into the Town. As of this writing, there
have been no interruptions in the service.

If you are new to town, or have not yet done so, sign-ups can be done online through the
website: www.whipcityliber.com/blandford or by going to the Town of Blandford website. The
cost for the service is only $85 per month, plus installation costs.

The Blandford MLP wishes to thank all of you for the tremendous support you have
given to this project!

Fiber is now available to 100% of homes in Blandford; Also, in partnership with
our ISP and the Towns of Otis, Becket and Washington (Southern Cluster) a redundant network
is now operational. This will insure that, in the event of an outage into any one town, the
network will automatically switch to another circuit. With this redundancy, the service to the
Town has been increased to 10 gigs.

Section 18.as follows; or take any action relative thereto:

Section 18, Temporary Moratorium. on the Construction of Large-Scale Ground Mounted Solar
Photovoltaic Installations and Battéry Energy Storage Systems.

A. Authority and Purpose

The Town. of Blandford currently has three approved large-scale. ground mounted solar
photovoltaic installations, commonly referred to as “solar farms”, completed or under
construction, two that are in the:permitting process and three more that have expressed interest
in pursuing permitting within the Town. Additionally, the Town is also aware of a large-scale
battery energy storage system that intends to seek permitting this year. Pursuant to the Zoning
By-laws, large scale ground mounted solar photovoltaic installations and battery energy storage
systems, are allowed pursuant to-site plan review and special permit in the Agricultural zoning
district, which includes approximately.90% of the Town’s land area. The Town is experiencing
unexpectedly high demand for large scale. solar facility special permits, and together with the
emphasis being placed on the development of solar energy to reduce dependence on fossil. fuels,
and the potential ‘availability of large areas of open space in the Town, it is anticipated that
applications to site latge scale solar facilities and battery energy storage systems in the Town
will continue unabated. The unexpected high demand has demonstrated the potential for rapidly
changing the face of the Town, thereby raising unanticipated legal, planning, and economic
issues and creating an urgent need to regulate the use.

At the time of adoption of the Town’s current Zoning By-Laws, the scope and scale of potential
energy development in Blandford was limited by existing grid constraints, particularly the
capacity of Eversource’s transmission and distribution infrastructure. The Town has since
iearned of and is responding to.a substantial substation expansion project proposed and permitted
by Eversource within the Town, intended to increase the system hosting capacity of the regional
grid. According to utility studies associated with the Plainfield-Blandford Group Study, the
existing transmission and distribution facilities in the area do not have sufficient hosting capacity
to accommodate the volume of Distributed Generation (DG), including solar and battery projects.
currently proposed or anticipated. The substation and related distribution upgrades are explicitly

42

designed to resolve these limitations, thereby enabling a significant increase in the number and
scale of new interconnections.

The expanded capacity of the upgraded substation is expected to make Blandford more attractive
to Commercial energy developers seeking special permits or other local approvals. The. Town’s
current zoning framework does not adequately account for the likely volume, intensity, and
cumulative impacts of such development, nor does it reflect the modern technological,
environmental, and land use implications of utility-scale battery energy storage systems or co-
located renewable installations.

Notably, when the Town’s zoning provisions were last adopted or amended, the regional
infrastructure improvements: now underway were entirely unknown and not reasonably
foreséeable. As a result, large portions of the Town, including approximately 90% of its land
area, remain potentially open to. cOmmiercial-scale energy development under outdated and
insufficiently protective standards.

The Town seeks to. avoid unintended consequences of unchecked or poorly regulated commercial
solar and battery energy storage development while it undertakes a deliberate and comprehensive
planning process: This process will include public input, expert consultation, and the
development of zoning amendments that ensure any future energy development is consistent with
the Towns land use goals, environmental protections, rural character, infrastructure limitations,
public safety, and State regulations.

Therefore, this temporary moratorium is necessary, reasonable, and narrowly tailored to. serve
the public interest while the Town updates its bylaws to responsibly manage a rapidly evolving.
energy landscape. The moratorium will not apply to residential roof mounted or small or medium
ground mounted solar installations.

Together with the increase in demand, the law concerning the ability of municipalities to regulate.
solar energy facilities and battery energy storage systems through its zoning bylaws has evolved
substantially by way of recent court decisions and Attorney General review of. proposed
amendments to those. bylaws. The Town’s recent attempts to addréss the impact of large-scale
solar facilities and battery energy storage systems through amendments to its Zoning By-laws
have been only partially successful, due in large part to the changes in the law. The Town needs
time to stady the impact of this evolution on its current regulation of these facilities and adjust
that ‘regulation as ‘necessary to ensure that it conforms to legal requirements while adequately
addressing the impact of large-scale solar facilities and larger battery energy storage systems on
the Town and its planning goals. Imposition of a temporary moratorium on the Construction of
Large-Scale Ground Mounted Solar Photovoltaic Installations and Battery Energy Storage.
Systems will allow sufficient time to assess these issues and amend the Zoning By-laws to
conform to legal requirements and address the impact of these facilities on the Town's
environmental resources and its planning goals.
